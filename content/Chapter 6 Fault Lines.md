I burst into tears.
Not the quiet kind. Not the controlled kind. The kind that racked my whole body, that made my shoulders shake and my breath come in gasps. I pressed my free hand over my mouth, trying to stop it, trying to be quiet, but I couldn't.

Dr. Patel didn't rush me. He stood beside the bed, patient and still, giving me the time I didn't know I needed.
Alex watched me. His thumb moved against my knuckles. Slow. Weak. But there.
"I'm okay," he tried to say. His voice was barely a whisper, raw and broken.
I shook my head. "You're not okay. You almost died. You scared me. You scared me so bad, Alex."
He didn't argue. He just kept looking at me with those heavy, clouded eyes.
"I'm sorry," he mouthed. No sound came out.
I squeezed his hand tighter. "Don't. Don't be sorry. Just—just stay. Please. Just stay."
Dr. Patel stepped forward then, gently checking Alex's pulse, his pupils, the monitors beside the bed. "He's stable," he said to me. "He's going to be okay. But we're going to keep him here for a few days. Observation. And a psychiatric consult."
I nodded. I didn't care what it took. I didn't care how long.
Alex's eyes fluttered closed again—not unconscious, just exhausted. His hand stayed in mine.
"Don't go," he whispered.
"Never," I said.
And I sat back down in the plastic chair, held his hand, and watched him breathe.




He stayed in the hospital for two more days.
Not because his body was still in danger—Dr. Patel had made that clear by the second morning. His heart was fine. His breathing was fine. The marks on his arms were healing, already scabbing over, already starting that slow, ugly process of becoming scars. Physically, he was stable.
But observation meant more than just watching his vitals.
It meant watching him.
And what they saw—what I saw—was a man who had come too close to the edge and was still trying to find his way back.

The first day, he slept most of the time.
The combination of exhaustion and medication kept him under for hours at a stretch. I sat in the plastic chair beside his bed, reading a magazine I wasn't really reading, drinking coffee from the hospital cafeteria that was too bitter and too hot. Sometimes I held his hand. Sometimes I just watched the rise and fall of his chest, reassuring myself that it hadn't stopped again.
When he was awake, he was quiet. Not the heavy, suffocating quiet from before. Something softer. Something tired.
He didn't want to talk about what happened. I didn't push.
We watched daytime television together—game shows and talk shows and commercials for things we didn't need. I told him about Angus, who had been fed by the neighbour and was probably sleeping on his slipper right now. He smiled at that. A small smile. But it was there.
I stayed until visiting hours ended. I kissed his forehead. I told him I'd be back in the morning.
He squeezed my hand and closed his eyes.


The second day was different.
He was more awake. More present. The fog had lifted some, though his eyes were still shadowed, still carrying something heavy behind them.
Dr. Patel came by in the morning. He talked to Alex about the psychiatric consult—a woman named Dr. Reeves who would be coming to see him later that day. Alex nodded along, not arguing, not resisting. That was new.
"A psychiatrist isn't a punishment," Dr. Patel said gently. "It's just another kind of help. Like a mechanic for the brain."
Alex almost laughed at that. Almost.
After Dr. Patel left, I pulled my chair closer to the bed. I took his hand.
"How are you feeling?" I asked.
He thought about it for a long time.
"Like I got hit by a truck," he said finally. "But also like… I don't know. Like someone turned the volume down. Just a little." "That's good," I said.
"It's something," he agreed.
We sat in silence for a while. The monitor beeped. The room was warm.
"Maria," he said. "Yeah?" "I don't want to do that again." "Which part?"
"Any of it. The shower. The marks. The—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I don't want to almost die again."
I felt my throat tighten.
"Then don't," I said. "Let us help you. Let me help you. Let Dr. Heart and Dr. Reeves and whoever else you need. Just don't try to do it alone anymore."
He looked at me. Really looked at me. For the first time since the bathroom, his eyes weren't hollow. "I'll try," he said.
"That's all I'm asking."

I visited him every day.

Both days. Morning and evening. I brought him clean clothes from home—his favorite sweatshirt, the soft one with the worn-out cuffs. I brought him a photo of Angus, which he propped up on the bedside table. I brought him coffee that wasn't from the cafeteria, smuggled in from the shop around the corner.
He drank it slowly, holding the cup with both hands, looking almost human again.
Dr. Reeves came and went. Alex didn't tell me everything they talked about—he didn't have to. But he told me enough.
"She said I've been surviving for sixteen years with a broken toolkit. That self-harm was the only tool I had for a long time. And that it's not my fault I kept using it. But now I have to learn new ones."
"That sounds right," I said.
"It sounds exhausting," he said.
"It will be. But you won't do it alone."
He reached over and took my hand. Squeezed it.
"I know," he said.

On the third morning, they discharged him.

Dr. Patel signed the papers. Dr. Reeves scheduled an outpatient appointment for the following week. Alex's arms were still bandaged, but the gauze was thinner now, and underneath, the marks were healing.
I drove him home.
He sat in the passenger seat with his hands in his lap, staring out the window at the trees and the houses and the ordinary world that had kept spinning while he was inside that hospital room.
When I pulled into the driveway, he didn't move right away.
"You okay?" I asked.
He looked at the house. At the porch. At the window where Angus was sitting, watching us.
"I'm scared," he said. "Of what?"
"Of going back inside. Of tonight. Of 3 a.m. What if it happens again? What if I can't stop it next time?"
I turned off the engine. I turned to face him.
"Then we call Dr. Heart. We call the crisis line. We call 911 again if we have to. You're not the same person you were three days ago, Alex. You have more tools now. And you have me. You're not alone in the dark anymore."
He was quiet for a long moment.
Then he opened the car door. Got out. Walked toward the house.
I followed.
Angus meowed loudly from the window. Alex laughed—a real laugh, small and rusty, but real.
"I missed you too, you fat orange jerk," he said through the glass.
I unlocked the door. Angus wound around our ankles. The house smelled like home.
Alex stood in the living room, looking around at the couch, the blanket, the coffee cups still on the table from the night everything changed.
"Different," he said quietly. "What is?"
"The house. It feels different. Like it survived something too."
I put my hand on his back. "We all did."
He nodded. Took a breath. Let it out.
"Okay," he said. "Okay. Let's figure out dinner. And then maybe we call Dr. Heart. And then maybe we just… sit. And see what happens." "That sounds like a plan," I said.
He smiled. Small. Fragile. But real.
And for the first time in days, I believed we might actually be okay.

A few days had passed.
Alex was okay.

Not _fine_—that word felt wrong now, too small, too flimsy for everything that had happened. But okay. Present. Breathing. Eating meals without being reminded. Getting out of bed in the morning and getting back into it at night.
The bandages came off on a Tuesday.
I watched him unwrap them slowly, standing in front of the bathroom mirror, his sleeves pushed up to his elbows. The marks underneath were still visible—raised, pink, some of them still scabbed—but they were healing. His skin was doing what skin did. Closing itself back together.
He stared at his arms for a long time.
Then he pulled his sleeves down and walked out of the bathroom.
He didn't say anything. Neither did I.


The days found a rhythm.
He went back to therapy—Dr. Heart twice a week now, and Dr. Reeves once a week for the medication management they'd started at the hospital. He took the pills she prescribed, small white ones in the morning and smaller blue ones at night. He didn't complain about them. He just swallowed and moved on.
I watched him for signs of slipping. The long showers. The silence. The way he used to pull away when I reached for him.
But the long showers stopped. The silence became something softer—not heavy, just quiet. And when I reached for him, he reached back.

One evening, we were sitting on the couch.
Angus was curled in Alex's lap, purring like a motorboat. Alex's hand moved slowly over his orange fur, back and forth, back and forth. A movie played on the screen—something we'd seen before, something safe.
"You know what's weird?" he said. "What?" "I don't miss it." "The marks?"
"The feeling. The urge. It's still there sometimes—like a whisper in the next room. But I don't _want_ to listen to it anymore. For the first time in months, I don't want to."
I leaned my head against his shoulder.
"That's not weird," I said. "That's progress."
He was quiet for a moment.
"I keep waiting for the other shoe to drop. For the noise to come back. For 3 a.m. to feel like it did that night."
I took his hand. His free hand. The one not buried in cat fur.
"Maybe it will," I said. "And maybe it won't. But either way, you're not facing it alone. That's the difference, Alex. That's the thing that changed."
He turned his head and looked at me. His eyes weren't hollow anymore. They were tired, yes. Wary, maybe. But there was something else there too.
Something that looked like hope.
"I love you," he said. "I love you too," I said.
Angus purred The movie played on.
And the evening stretched out in front of us—ordinary, unremarkable, full of small mercies.
Alex was okay. And for now, that was enough.
